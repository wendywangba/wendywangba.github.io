Rebuilding a join path that didn't exist

Off-grid solar nonprofit, Colombia · PostgreSQL, Python (pandas, networkx), Tableau
Miller Center consulting engagement — 2nd place university-wide

49,207Records
24Tables, no docs
1 weekRaw db → pitch

The question the client wanted answered was simple: which of our field agents are actually performing? The obstacle was not analytical. It was that the payments table's user_id column was empty for every single row — so there was no way to connect a payment to the agent who had brought that customer in. The database also arrived with no data dictionary and no ER diagram.

Reconstructing that link was the project. I wrote profiling scripts to generate the missing data dictionary table by table, then inferred the foreign keys programmatically — scanning for *_id columns shared between tables and treating an overlap of five or more values as a candidate relationship, then rendering the result as an ER graph with networkx. Agent names were spelled inconsistently across tables, so I normalised them with NFKD to strip accents and built a canonical name map on top. In Tableau I duplicated the users and lead_generators tables to complete a join path that the schema could not otherwise express. Every relationship I inferred, I checked against row counts and sampled records before building on it.

Once payments could be attributed, volume and quality turned out to be different axes. The agent recovering the most money was not the one with the best payment success rate, and the highest rate of all sat on a portfolio too small to mean anything. Ranking on amount recovered therefore rewards the wrong behaviour. I recommended coaching targets be set on success and on-time rates instead and delivered a reusable KPI framework; the judging panel asked me to extend it into a single effectiveness index.

Two things I chose not to do are the parts I'd defend hardest. There were only about fifteen agents and seven with meaningful transaction volume, so after trying exploratory clustering I dropped it — the sample could not support a model, and a descriptive KPI framework with explicit business rules was the honest answer. And the language field, which mattered because the client serves Wayuunaiki-speaking communities, was populated for 3 of 932 customers. Rather than analyse it anyway, we wrote "start collecting village-level language data" into the recommendations.

Twenty-seven years of satellite data, reduced to one comparison

Six-month practicum with the research team at X, the Moonshot Factory (Alphabet)
Python (xarray), BigQuery, Looker Studio

1997–2025Record length
10,000+Daily NetCDF grids

I owned the water-clarity workstream on a four-person blue-economy research team. The source was a public Copernicus Marine satellite archive — most days cloud-obscured or off-target. The pipeline I built in xarray extracted three coastal sites from every usable day, producing the analytical dataset behind the team's dashboard, delivered through BigQuery.

Exposed coastline sat at 2.3× the turbidity of sheltered coastline, consistently, across twenty-seven years. I presented this to senior public-sector stakeholders, and a senior environmental official confirmed it matched what their field teams already knew — which is the outcome I wanted: the satellite record independently reproduced operational knowledge that had never been quantified, which is what makes the rest of the archive worth trusting.

A course-advising bot that refuses to guess

RAG over a university department's advising documents · 3-person team
React + TypeScript front end, Python backend

Students kept asking the same prerequisite questions because the answers were scattered across checklists, flow charts and spreadsheet footnotes. We built a retrieval-augmented advising bot over those documents. I owned the product design, the chat front end, and the answer policy; my teammates built the retrieval pipeline.

The design decision I care about is what it does when it doesn't know. An advising bot that invents a prerequisite is worse than no bot — a student acts on it and takes the wrong course. So the policy is strict grounding: if the answer isn't in the source documents it declines rather than generating something plausible. I built the demo around that, including answering Chinese-language questions from English-only sources, and surfacing prerequisites that were buried in footnotes.
